Chapter 8: The Compass and The Bloodline
The year was 1991, and spring had settled gently over the Coachella Valley. － The desert was warming back up, palm fronds drifted easy in the afternoon breeze, and folks were beginning to settle into the rhythm of another year. － Out on the highways, though, there was a different kind of movement. － Convoys of buses and flights of young servicemen were bringing another wave of Operation Desert Storm troops home. － Yellow ribbons fluttered from mailboxes and storefront windows, American flags caught the breeze, and families gathered anywhere they thought they might catch that first glimpse of someone they'd been praying over for months. You could see it before a single word was spoken. － Boys climbed off those buses carrying duffel bags, but what really weighed them down wasn't packed in canvas. － Some smiled because the people waiting on them needed it. － Others stared off toward some place only they couldn't unsee. － Mothers cried. － Fathers hugged harder than they ever had before. － Wives held on like they were making up for every day apart. I'd seen that look before. － Twenty years earlier it had another name, another jungle, another set of uniforms. Different war. － Same thousand-yard gaze. Watching those boys find their families, I couldn't help but think about Bubba. War always changes its clothes, but it never changes its appetite. The garage sat half-open that afternoon, the dry heat sliding in like it owned the place. － My old ceiling fan turned lazy overhead, chopping through air thick enough to chew on. － I moved slow. － Time does that when you stop trying to outrun it. － The work becomes ritual, and the ritual becomes the thing that keeps your hands busy while your mind wanders roads it swore it was done traveling. Outside, under a faded tarp, Emerald Thunder rested in her usual silence. － Even covered, I could trace every line of that old 1960 Chevy K-10 without lifting the canvas. － She'd carried memories heavier than engines. Lately, looking at her felt different. Hell, looking at myself felt different nowadays. Just turned forty this year. Had my checkup last week, and doc said I should start taking my health more seriously. I think I'm at the point of taking him up on that advice, because I tell you what... as time goes on? I'm movin' slower... And the slower I'm movin'? The faster the clock moves... － My hands were elbow-deep in an engine bay, yankin' on a alternator, stained with old grease and even older habits, when the phone on the garage wall started ringing. I looked at the clock... Yup... One thirty in the afternoon, right on time. See, my pal Bugsy? The same Bugsy from the Speed 'n Lube? ...That cat's still around, doin the same ol thing in Indio. He calls at least once a week to pick my brain about how to fix something or another down at his shop. The guy's been in the business over twenty years. At this point? I think he's just lookin for conversation with an old friend... So? ...I wiped my palms on a rag that had probably survived more years than most conversations, walked over and picked up the receiver.

"Talk to me, Bugsy." He didn't answer with his usual swagger. "G-Mack..." he said quietly. － "You sitting down? － I hope so, I... I don't even know how to tell you this." I thought to myself, "Oh, boy... What did he break now?"... I chuckled a bit... "Ha, I been sitting since before you called, man. － Just spit it out. － We gettin' too old for secrets." He let out a slow breath. "Okay, well... Lady rolled through the shop today, right? － Blew a tire clean apart. － We got to talking... － neighbors... － old times... － I didn’t recognize her at first." He paused. "And, G... it's Susie Anderson." ...that name didn't come back like a memory. It rolled through me like a wave that had been crossing the ocean for twenty-five years before finally reaching shore. Susie Anderson. Fourth of July, 1966. The park fountain. The girl who looked at Benny, heard me wrong, smiled that smile, and asked... "Who's Bubba?" That one little question. One little nickname. And somehow it stayed with him from California streets all the way to the jungles of Vietnam. “Susie…” I said, my voice softening, the streetwise mask slipping off completely. “Man, Bugsy… that’s a different lifetime. That's the girl who gave him the name. The one from the fountain.” “Yeah,” Bugsy muttered, his voice dropping an octave, hesitant. “Yeah, it is. But G-Mack… it gets deeper. Susie was talking about family stuff. Neighborhood stuff. Said her dad took a job at a factory in Oakland, so they moved away shortly after Bubba left, and she’s been there ever since. Grew up, got her own place there...” I interrupted Bugsy. “Okay man, slow down... What are you gettin' at with all this?” “Alright G-Mack... she says… man, she says she’s got a daughter. Twenty-four years old. Born in '67, right after… well, right after Benny shipped out to 'Nam.... The air in the garage changed texture. Goosebumps went straight up my back. “Yeah? Go on, man, what else?” I said quiet. “She… she showed me a picture, G-Mack,” Bugsy stumbled over the words, his breath catching. “And I’m looking at it, and I’m just… I’m seeing him, man. It ain't just close. It ain't just a resemblance. It’s… it’s Bubba's daughter. Same eyes. Same smile. Same way he looks like he’s listening to something you can’t hear. And G... The girl, her name? her name is Lauren Azure... － Azure. The color blue. Just a different label on paper that was never strong enough to hold what it covered. Same blood in the ink. I looked out toward the tarp-covered truck, not yet knowing how deep any of this really ran. All I knew right then was that Benjamin Buford Blue... My boy Benny... Had a piece of himself still roaming this Earth. It wasn't just an old memory anymore. It was a call to move. “Bugsy,” I said, my voice steady, carrying the full weight of a man who finally knew his destination. “Yeah, G?” “I want to find her. I need to talk to Lauren myself. Oakland or not, I've gotta get in touch with her. I just have to... You get it, right?” There was a pause on the line, Bugsy letting that settle in. Then his voice came back, lower, cautious. “But you don’t even know where she is in Oakland, G.” I exhaled slow through my nose, eyes still locked on the tarp outside. “I’m gonna have to come up with something,” I said. A beat of silence held between us, thick and unfinished. “Alright,” Bugsy said finally. “I'll get in touch with you later, G-Mack... And hey...” － “Thanks buddy.” － “Hey, don't mention it, G.... That's what brothers are for, right?” ...We got off the phone...

－ Right then, for a moment, the garage felt louder without the phone than it did with it. Outside, past the open bay, I thought about what I’d seen earlier that day—those Desert Storm boys stepping off buses, finding arms that were waiting for them. Daughters with smiles from ear to ear... greeting their fathers... their heroes... coming home from their tour of duty. Dads picking their daughters up off the ground, and instantly... you’d be fooled for just a second, like everything is fine... They were the fortunate ones... － Some made it back whole. － Some came back carrying things nobody could see. － But they came back. Bubba didn’t. Vietnam didn’t give him that chance. And somewhere in that gap between return and absence, something settled in me heavier than grease or steel ever could. He never came home to Susie... He never knew he had a daughter to meet... He never knew of the respectable, stand-up man his father Bernard was... That part of him never got a landing. Just open water and unfinished distance. I looked back toward Emerald Thunder under her tarp. Not a machine anymore. A reminder. Whatever road I was on, it didn’t end where I thought it did. － It bent toward something older than me, something unfinished that still had breath in it somewhere in this world. I exhaled slow. Not thinking. Just knowing. The phone rang again. Sharp. Immediate. Like it hadn’t left at all. "Hey, G... Bugsy again." "What'd you forget?" － "She paid with a check...." － "Her address is printed on it..." － Another pause. "2450 Montgomery Street. Oakland." He chuckled softly. "And G... Susie said her daughter Lauren runs a record shop right across the street from her house." Everything went still. Not empty. Still. The kind of stillness that comes right before a man finally knows which direction he's supposed to walk. I closed my eyes. The map drew itself. "Alright," I said quietly. "I got it." ... I woke rhe next morning, ready to go. The drive from the Coachella Valley to the Bay was a long, dusty meditation. Emerald Thunder didn’t complain once; she just ate up the miles, her engine humming a steady, rhythmic mantra that synced with the pulse in my own temples. 1991 was shifting under my tires, the golden glare of the desert fading into the cool, damp embrace of Northern California. Forty-one years old, and for the first time in a lifetime, I felt like I was driving toward a ghost that hadn't finished its business. When I rolled into Oakland, the air felt different—thicker, smelling of salt and wet asphalt instead of sand and sagebrush. I found Montgomery Street easy enough, a row of modest houses that looked like they held their own secrets. Across the way, a small storefront stood with a sign that simply read Azure Sounds. My heart hammered a erratic rhythm against my ribs, a sound louder than the idling K-10. I stepped out, the air cooling my skin. I pushed the door open, the bell overhead giving a sharp, loud jingle. Then, I heard it. It wasn't just a sound;

it was a memory clawing its way back from the grave. A loud chuckle—mischievous, warm, and utterly unmistakable. Bubba. It was his laugh, echoing in a shop that smelled of dust and vinyl. I froze, my hand still on the door handle. A woman was leaning over the counter, helping a guy find a blues record. The way she tilted her head, the way she squinted when she was thinking, the casual, easy way she leaned against the laminate—it was like seeing a photograph come to life in three dimensions. I moved to the back, fingers trailing over the spine of a record I wasn't really reading. I listened. Every sentence she spoke carried the same cadence, the same slight drawl that Benny had carried all the way to the jungle. She had his jaw, his eyes, that same way of listening like she was tapped into a frequency nobody else could hear. When the customer finally clicked his tongue and walked out, the silence in the shop turned heavy. I stood there, feeling the weight of the last twenty-five years pressing down on my shoulders. She walked over, her steps light, and stopped a few feet away. "You finding everything okay, or do you need a hand with the inventory?" I looked at the rack, then back at her. "Just browsing," I said, my voice feeling raspy. I gestured toward a stack of shiny, plastic-encased discs near the register. "Can you believe these things? Compact discs." I shook my head, a dry smile touching my lips. "One little scratch on the surface, and the whole thing is ruined. Ain't no soul in it. This technology won't last long, mark my words." She laughed again—that same, beautiful, haunting laugh. "You're probably right. I think I'll stick to the wax for now." I let the moment hang. Then, I decided to stop dancing. "Look... Lauren, right?" She blinked, her posture shifting from professional to cautious, guarded. "Umm, yeah? But how do you know? Have I met you before?" "No, you haven't," I said, looking her dead in the eye. "But I came all the way from Palm Springs to chat with you about something. Something we both have in common." I paused, gauging her, seeing the hesitation flicker behind those familiar eyes. "What time do you close up shop? And, would you be willing to chat with me a minute?" She didn't retreat. Instead, she took a beat, looking me over like she was trying to solve a puzzle. "Umm, yeah, no... I mean... Yeah, that's fine. I'm open till five o'clock. I get outta here a little after." "Alright," I said, a wave of relief washing over me. "You drink coffee? Noticed a shop down on the corner." "Yeah... That sounds just fine," she replied, still looking at me with that intense, inherited curiosity. I started to turn toward the door, then stopped. "Hold on though... You got a name, or what?" I smiled. "Yeah, kiddo, everybody calls me Big G—" I stopped. The words caught in my throat. For a second, I felt like a boy again, standing in a garage in the desert with a scarred door panel and a bus ticket in a pocket. I didn't know why I was stopping, why the name felt too big, too heavy for this moment. I looked at her, saw the girl who was the living, breathing bridge to everything I’d lost, and something settled in my chest. "The name's Greg, kiddo," I said softly. "Just call me Greg." She smiled, a slow, gentle expression that made the room feel a little less cold. "Alright, 'Greg'... You've got me real curious. I'll see you around five-thirty, down at the coffee shop." I stepped out into the Oakland afternoon, the bell ringing behind me one more time. I walked back to Emerald Thunder, my hands steady. The road had been long, but for the first time, I knew exactly where I was going.


Chapter 9: The Name She Never Knew

The coffee shop wasn't anything special. A little place tucked between old brick buildings, the kind of spot where the same folks sat in the same chairs every morning, reading the same newspapers and arguing about the same things they had been arguing about for twenty years. The kind of place that didn't care who you were when you walked in. It just gave you a cup of coffee and a quiet corner. And that's exactly what I needed. Because some conversations ain't meant to happen standing up. Some conversations need a table between two people. A little time. A little room for the world to stop spinning. I sat there waiting, hands wrapped around a coffee cup that had gone mostly untouched. Outside the window, Oakland moved along like it always did. Cars passing. People walking. Life happening. Funny thing about life... The world can keep moving even when yours feels like it stopped. Then the bell above the door rang. And there she was. Lauren. I watched her look around until she spotted me. She gave a small smile and walked over. She still had that same curious look in her eyes. That same way Bubba used to look when he was trying to figure something out. Like the world was a puzzle, and he was just waiting for somebody to hand him the missing pieces. She sat down across from me. "Hey, Greg." "Hey, kiddo." For a second, neither one of us said anything. Because what do you say when you're about to change somebody's entire understanding of where they came from? I took a slow breath. "Lauren... before I say anything, I want you to know something." She leaned forward slightly. "Okay..." "I didn't come here to bring you some sad story. I didn't come here to reopen some old wound. I'm here because I believe some things belong to people... even if it takes twenty-five years to finally find their way home." She looked confused, but she listened. And that was something Bubba would've appreciated. That boy always said the most important thing a person could do was listen. "Back in 1966," I said, "your mother met somebody." Her expression changed. Not fear. Not yet. Just curiosity. "Somebody who changed my life." I looked down at my coffee for a second. "His name was Benjamin Buford Blue." Her eyes narrowed slightly. "Benjamin...?" I nodded. "Yeah. But nobody called him that." A tiny smile crossed my face. "Everybody called him Bubba." The name hung there between us. And then I saw it. A little flicker. Like something in her already knew. "My mom knew him?" "Yeah, she did." I leaned back. "Your mom was the first person who ever called him Bubba." Lauren froze. "Wait... what?" I laughed quietly. "Yeah. That name everybody knew him by? That came from her." I told her about that summer day. The park. The fountain. A young girl named Susie Anderson. A nervous teenage boy who didn't know what to say. A stupid joke from his best friend that accidentally created a name that would follow him the rest of his life. And Lauren just sat there, taking it all in. "My mom gave him that name..." "She sure did." I smiled. "And the funny thing is... from the moment Bubba saw her, he was gone." She laughed softly. "Gone?" "Oh yeah. Completely." I shook my head. "That boy could talk for three hours about shrimp boats, engines, fishing, or some crazy idea he had... but when Susie walked into the room? Suddenly Benjamin Buford Blue forgot how words worked." That got a real laugh out of her. A little piece of Bubba coming through. Then her smile faded. "Greg..." "Yeah?" "I always wondered." Her voice got quieter. "I wondered who my dad was." There it was. The question that had probably lived in her heart her whole life. Not anger. Not resentment. Just a missing piece. "I didn't know if he left. I didn't know if he didn't want me. I just... I never knew." I nodded slowly. "Well, kiddo..." I reached into my jacket pocket. "I think it's time you meet him." I pulled out an old photograph. The edges were worn. The kind of wear that doesn't come from being forgotten. It comes from being held. She took it carefully. And when she looked down... She stopped. The picture was me and Bubba as teenagers. Two dumb kids standing beside an old car, both of us grinning like we owned the world. Lauren stared. "That's him?" "That's him." She looked closer. "Wow..." "What?" She looked up. "He looks like me." I smiled. "Yeah." She looked back at the picture. "The eyes." "Yep." "The smile." "Yep." She shook her head. "That's crazy." I leaned back. "No, kiddo. That's family." And then I told her about him. Not just the big things. Not just the war. Not just the tragedy. I told her about the real Bubba. The kid who could make anybody laugh. The guy who would give you the shirt off his back and apologize because he didn't have a second shirt to give you. The man who believed everybody deserved respect. The man who loved her mother. The man who never knew he had a daughter. And that was the part that hurt the most. "He would've loved you." She looked down. "You think so?" I didn't hesitate. "I know so." The coffee shop got quiet. Not empty. Just quiet. The kind of quiet where something important has been said. After a while, I leaned forward. "Lauren..." She looked up. "I want you to understand something. You lost a father you never got to meet. I lost a brother I never got to say goodbye to." I paused. "But neither one of us has to carry that alone anymore." Her eyes got a little wet. I smiled. "So here's the deal. You got a whole lifetime of questions, and I got a whole lifetime of stories." She laughed softly. "That sounds like a lot of stories." I grinned. "Kid, you have no idea." I tapped the table. "So whenever you need somebody to talk to... whenever you want to hear about your dad... you got me." I looked her straight in the eye. "And if you're okay with it... you can consider me family." A small smile crossed her face. "Family?" "Yeah." I shrugged. "You can just call me Uncle G." She looked down, smiling. Then back at me. "I'd like that." She took a breath. "Uncle G." That one got me. After twenty-five years of carrying a memory, hearing that name felt like something settling back into place. Then she smiled. "But if I'm family..." She tilted her head. "You can't keep calling me Lauren like I'm some stranger." I laughed. "Alright then. What do I call you?" She smiled. "Roxy." I nodded. "Roxy it is." And just like that... The past found a new name. The drive back to Palm Springs was quieter than the drive up. Not sad quiet. Different. The kind of quiet where your mind is sorting through a thousand thoughts at once.
