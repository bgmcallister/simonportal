Chapter 1: Desert Roots and the Deep Green Gift  
   
Everyone knows "Bubba" by now... The shrimp-loving friend of Forrest Gump, who died on the edge of a river in Vietnam...  
  
Well... Long before the war... Before he fell in love with shrimp and seafood... Before he met that skinny kid named Gump at basic training... There was "Benny Blue"... and the Coachella Valley, of California...
—

...—Hey folks, let me introduce myself. The name's Gregory DeShawn McAllister, but out here? Well... Everybody in the valley just know me as Big G-Mack. Born in 1950. Palm Springs, California...  
  
Where the valley was an oven, and the streets smelled of hot asphalt and thirsty ambition.  
  
Pops, Harold, put his soul into his family. He'd lay bricks all day in Coachella Valley, his hands rough as desert stone. Mama Ernestine held the house down like a queen guarding her throne.  
  
Life back then was a little different for us black working-class families—especially in a place like Palm Springs and the Coachella Valley. When most people think of Palm Springs? They think of the classy life, right?. Big swimming pools? VIP dining? Expensive shopping? Maybe catching a glimpse of a celebrity on vacation...  
  
North of the city, though? You had the gears of the civilization—a community all on its own, full of working-class families and the true soul of the resort town... families of all different ethnicities, from all walks of life.  
  
North Palm Springs and Desert Hot Springs... The very dust of our dirt roads breathes of dignity and determination, and the pride of lives well earned.  
  
To me, I couldn't be more proud to call that home.  
  
Back then? Things were golden, man... Hot summers, Schwinn bikes with chains that smelled of grease and adventure... radios bumpin' The Temptations, and a sky so wide you could almost lose yourself in it, if it wasn't for the heat of the sun reminding you of why you don't...  
  
My childhood was amazing, and that's mostly 'cause of my best buddy Benjamin. See, Benny was four months older, born in '49. I tell you what—that kid was my mentor... my big brother, in everything but blood, of course. His mama, Miss Loretta, ran an empire, raisin' six kids, yet somehow let me slip under her wing too.  
  
See, Benny's old man wasn't in the picture anymore. We didn't really talk about it much, but I knew it was on his mind at times... My dad was friends with Benny's dad when we were young... That's how Benny and I met. Back then, our dads worked at the San Diego docks. They'd ride together every workday, and they'd lay brick together on the weekends. When Pops started laying brick full-time, he hired a few other guys, while Benny's dad started fixing cars on the weekends. I guess they just naturally fell out of touch for a while.  
  
But Benny started coming around more often... At some point, he became just another member of the family.  
  
Our moms, trying to keep us in line while waitressing together at the diner, forged a friendship that also echoed into our lives. Before long, Benny wasn't just my best friend. He was family. We were all family. We looked out for each other... The way it should be..
  
And Pops? Benny trulylooked up to him like a second father. He practically modeled himself after my old man. He absorbed his lessons and Embodied his morals... 

—When he didn't have his own father to learn from? Pops was always there, with a stern voice, and a heart twice as big.  
  
Family dinners? Skillet-frying catfish we'd pull from the canal every week. The smell of hot fish and butter, thick in the air like it owned the room. Benny and I would fish all day, bring home the catch, and the whole house licked their lips. Ain't nothing beat that sizzle, that first bite that made your knees weak.  
  
Me and Benny dreamed of startin' a seafood spot. We were gonna make it big. We fell in love with fishing while taking our ride-along trips with Benny's uncle Felix on his shrimping boat. Prices on lobster and shrimp kept us waitin', but man, we believed.  
  
See, every one of those big dreams we had traces back to the little sparks...

And we all know what sparks can do out in the dry desert...

We were fifteen when Pops handed us the keys to Emerald Thunder, a deep green 1960 Chevy K-10 pickup. I'll never forget the first time I saw her gleaming in the afternoon haze. I turned to Pops and gave him the biggest hug I had ever given in my life.  
  
Benny was so excited, he was practically stuttering.  
  
Pops had worked night shifts for weeks to save every penny.  
  
That truck—it was finally ours.  
  
Dad didn't want us driving until we got our license, of course. But Benny and I? We were fifteen,and we hadnbig dreams. We already made big plans for her within a couple hours.  
  
That same night, Pops went into work.  
  
He never came home.  
  
A car accident stole him the very day he gave us that truck, leaving Emerald Thunder a vessel of memories, lessons, and love I could never repay.  
  
And Benny took it hard, too.  
  
He'd lost more than a friend's father that night.  
  
  
  
  
CHAPTER 2: After the Dust Settled  
  
For Benny? Pops was about as close to a father as he'd had in years. He didn't say a whole lot after the funeral.  
  
Neither of us did.
  
We were still just kids, but suddenly? We felt a whole lot older than we wanted to be.
  
Mama kept everybody moving... lookin back? I think things hurt the most when I think of mama. Besides the fact that she just lost her true love... There were still meals to cook, bills to figure out, clothes to wash, and six million little things that don't stop just because somebody you love is gone.  
  
That's how life is, I guess.  
  
It doesn't ask if you're ready.  
  
It just keeps going.  
  
Benny was around more than ever after that. Nobody questioned it. He'd come through the back door, grab something to eat, and sit at the kitchen table like just another kid in the family.
  
Sometimes he'd help Mama with something before heading outside... but honestly? I think she just enjoyed having another person to talk with, or just vent to... 
Benny was good at that... Just listening. 
 
   In the days I felt things were getting too hard to push through, he'd just sit with me. Other times, we'd disappear into the garage and lose ourselves in whatever needed fixing.  
  
That garage had been Pops's classroom long before it became ours.  
  
He used to try teaching both of us little things about cars. How to listen to an engine. How to tell when something wasn't sitting right. Which tools to use, and which ones not to use unless you wanted to hear him hollering from the other side of the yard.  
  
I paid attention.  
  
Mostly.  
  
Benny?  
  
That boy soaked it all up.  
  
He'd watch Pops work like he was studying for a test nobody else knew about. If Pops showed us something once, Benny remembered it. Show him twice, and he'd probably be correcting me the next time I tried it.  
  
I used to get annoyed when he'd do that.  

"Man, I know how to do it."  
  
"You clearly don't."  
  
"I was getting there."  
  
"Well? You were getting there wrong."  
  
Then he'd take the wrench out of my hand and show me.  
  
The same thing Pops had shown us.  
  
The same little trick.  
  
The same way of explaining it.  
  
Looking back, I realize Benny wasn't just learning how to work on cars.  
  
He was learning from Pops.  
  
Learning the way Pops did things.  
  
And after Pops was gone, Benny started passing some of that knowledge on to me.  
  
We didn't talk about Pops much.  
  
Not because we didn't miss him.  
  
We just didn't know what to say.  
  
The first few weeks, every little thing reminded me of him.  
  
A pair of work gloves sitting where he'd left them.  
  
The sound of somebody's boots crossing the porch.  
  
The smell of grease on an old work shirt.  
  
Even Emerald Thunder.  
  
I'd look at that truck and remember the way Pops had smiled when he handed us those keys.  
  
Then I'd remember that he never came home.  
  
Benny had his own way of dealing with it.  
  
He worked.  
  
If something needed fixing, Benny fixed it. If something needed cleaning, he cleaned it. If Emerald Thunder needed attention, he was under the hood before I could even ask what he was doing.  
  
And sometimes, he'd stop what he was doing and show me something Pops had taught us.  
  
I'd watch him work, and every now and then, I'd swear I could hear Pops's voice coming out of his mouth.  
  
"Don't force it."  
  
"Listen to the engine."  
  
"Take your time."  
  
Little things.  
  
But those little things stuck with me.  
  
Maybe that was Benny's way of keeping Pops close.  
  
Maybe it was his way of keeping his mind busy.  
  
I never asked.  
  
We just kept moving.  
  
And slowly, the days started becoming less about what we'd lost and more about what we still had.  
  
The truck became our little escape.  
  
We'd spend hours working on her, arguing over what needed fixing, dreaming about where we'd take her once I finally got my license.  
  
Benny always had somewhere in mind.  
  
The beach.  
  
The mountains.  
  
San Diego.  
  
Anywhere that wasn't home.  
  
I'd laugh and tell him he was gonna spend more money on gas than we had between us.  
  
He'd just grin.  
  
"That's why we gotta get rich."  
  
"Doing what?"  
  
"Seafood."  
  
That boy never let that dream go.  
  
Uncle Felix had taught him plenty on those shrimping trips, and Benny soaked up every bit of it. We'd bring shrimp home whenever we could, and before long, Benny was studying recipes like they were schoolbooks.  
  
He'd sit around reciting them to me—shrimp this way, shrimp that way, boil it, fry it, grill it—like he was already running a restaurant in his head.  
  
I'd laugh at him.  
  
"Man, you ain't never gonna remember all that."  
  
He'd look at me dead serious.  
  
"I will."  
  
And he did.  
  
Shrimp, lobster, crab... He'd name off half the ocean like he already owned it.  
  
Maybe that's what made Benny different.  
  
He didn't just dream about things.  
  
He remembered them.  
  
Every recipe. Every fishing spot. Every little trick Uncle Felix taught him.  
  
And every little thing Pops had taught him about cars.  
  
Some things just stuck with that boy.  
  
And somehow, even after Pops died, those dreams didn't disappear.  
  
Maybe that's what we needed.  
  
Something to believe in.  
  
Something to look forward to.  
  
Something that belonged to us.  
  
By the time summer came around, the worst of the shock had started to settle.  
  
The pain didn't disappear.  
  
It just became something we carried differently.  
  
I got my license.  
  
Benny acted like he'd been waiting on it his whole life.  
  
The first thing we did was take Emerald Thunder out.  
  
Just the two of us.  
  
No Pops.  
  
No parents.  
  
Just two dumb fifteen-year-olds in a truck we were still too young to fully appreciate.  
  
We drove until the sun started dropping behind the mountains.  
  
Neither of us said much.  
  
We didn't need to.  
  
For a little while, it felt like nothing had changed.  
  
Then we turned around and went home.  
  
That's how those years went.  
  
Work.  
  
School.  
  
Fishing.  
  
Cars.  
  
Family.  
  
Dreams.  
  
And every once in a while, we'd find ourselves standing beside Emerald Thunder, talking about the future like it was something we could just drive toward.  
  
By the time we reached sixteen, Benny had changed.  
  
Not all at once.  
  
Nothing that dramatic.  
  
He was just growing into himself.  
  
More confident.  
  
More stubborn.  
  
More protective.  
  
Sometimes too damn protective.  
  
He'd started giving me advice like he was forty years old instead of sixteen.  
  
I'd tell him, "Man, you ain't my dad."  
  
He'd roll his eyes.  
  
"I know that."  
  
Then he'd give me the advice anyway.  
  
I'd laugh at him.  
  
He'd get mad.  
  
And ten minutes later, we'd be working on a carburetor together like nothing happened.  
  
That's just how Benny and I were.  
  
We could butt heads harder than anybody I'd ever known.  
  
But neither one of us stayed mad for long.  
  
Life had already taught us that some things mattered more than being right.  
  
And Benny?  
  
He mattered.  
  
That was the thing about growing up with somebody.  
  
You don't always notice the changes while they're happening.  
  
One day, you're two kids riding bikes through the neighborhood.  
  
Then suddenly you're sixteen, standing in a garage with grease on your hands, talking about girls, cars, money, fishing, recipes, and whatever the hell comes next.  
  
And somewhere along the way...  
  
Your best friend becomes the person you trust to have your back when you can't see what's coming.  
  
That's where we were heading.  
  
Toward the summer of 1966.  
  
The valley was hot.  
  
The garage was busy.  
  
And Benny Blue was about to become somebody else entirely.  
  
But neither of us knew that yet.  
  
  
  
  
  
CHAPTER 3: July Sparks and the Birth of a Moniker

July 4th, 1966.

The valley was hotter than hell that day... The garage was buzzing, and Benny and I were already getting on each other's nerves. It started with some older guys I'd been hanging around lately. Nothing I thought was a big deal. Benny didn't see it that way. Benny had been on me about them for days. “Those boys ain't your friends, Greg.” I rolled my eyes. “Man, I can take care of myself.” “That ain't the point,” he said. “Then what's the point?” “...You don't know what they're about, man.” I didn't like hearing it. Not because I thought Benny was completely wrong. I just didn't like him acting like he had to keep watch over me every second of the day.

Benny kept working on the engine while we argued. I handed him tools, he kept turning bolts, and somehow we both got louder. “You ain't gotta tell me who I can be friends with.” “I'm not trying to tell you who you can be friends with, you knucklehead.” “You sure sound like it.” “No, G. I'm trying to tell you who you shouldn't be friends with.” Benny's hand slipped while cranking on a bolt in the engine bay, and his knuckles thumped against the inside fender. “Yeah, who's the knucklehead now?” I shot him a cocky grin. Benny shook his hand and glared at me. “Man, I'm serious.” I wasn't quite as calm as he was. “You ain't gotta keep worrying about me all the damn time.” “Somebody's gotta.” That one got me quiet. I looked at him. Benny didn't look back. He just kept working on the engine. For a few seconds, neither of us said anything. The garage suddenly felt a whole lot quieter.

I finally sighed. “I know you're looking out for me. I do. But you gotta let me make my own mistakes.” Benny stopped what he was doing. He looked at me for a second. Then he nodded. “I know.” That was it. No big apology. No handshake. We just went back to work.

A little while later, Benny grabbed the keys to the Biscayne. He looked at me. “You drivin' or what?” I grinned. “Hell yeah.” So we took the Chevy out for a spin. The Biscayne gleamed in the afternoon sun as we rolled through town. We talked about cars, girls, fishing, and all the other important things two sixteen-year-old boys thought they knew everything about.

Then Benny saw her. Susie Anderson. Benny had been talking about Susie for weeks, but every time she got within twenty feet of him, suddenly the boy forgot how words worked. She was standing near the park with a group of friends, lighting off firecrackers. Fireworks were going off everywhere, so loud, it drowned out the music on the radio.

I slowed down. I had a genius idea. I looked over at Susie and her friends, then I looked over at Benny. I knew he wasn't gonna say anything to her... So I figured I'd help him out. I pulled the Biscayne over and opened the driver's door to get out of the car. Benny turned, looking a bit panicked. “Wait... G...What are you doing?” I turned to him and shot him a grin. “G...No! Don't do it...” Getting out of the Biscayne, I tapped the fender like it was his. Susie looked over at us.

“Man, this is one nice Chevy you got here!” I said loud enough for Susie to hear. “You take good care of this thing! I bet you paid a lot for it!” Benny looked at me like he was gonna kill me. Susie looked over. She smiled. “Hey guys! Whose Chevy is that!?” Before Benny could say a word, I pointed at him. “Oh, this car? It's my brother's car!” Right as I said the word “brother,” a whole pack of firecrackers went off nearby, making it even harder to hear what anybody was saying. Benny's eyes got wide. Susie tilted her head. “Wait, it's Bubba's car? Who's Bubba?”

I looked at Benny. Benny looked at me. He was already red in the face. Then he just shrugged. “That's me.” I damn near fell over laughing. Benny just shook his head. But Susie smiled. And somehow, that was enough. I handed Benny the keys and told him to take her home. He gave me that look again. The one that said he knew exactly what I'd done. I just grinned and started walking.

I didn't know it then, but Benny Blue had just become Bubba. For the rest of the night, I called him Bubba every chance I got. The funny part? It stuck.

I watched the Biscayne's taillights fade down Palm Canyon Drive, those red dots shrinking into the night like fireflies slipping through your fingers. By then, the air smelled like spent fireworks, gasoline, and hot pavement. The Fourth of July celebration was winding down, but every now and then another firework cracked somewhere in the distance.

I passed the Tramway Gas Station. Old Man Reyes was inside, leaning against the counter with a newspaper. He looked up and gave me a nod. I nodded back. Then I kept walking.

The dirt road home kicked up dust around my shoes. Off to the side, a wide scar of earth stretched across the desert where crews were building a new highway. Folks around town had started calling it the future road. It didn't look like much yet. Just dirt, machinery, dust, and a whole lot of empty desert. But everybody knew it was coming. Eventually, it would become Interstate 10. Back then, though, it was just a road being built across the desert.

I had no idea how much the valley was about to change. None of us did. That future road eventually arrived. And with it came the spring of 1967.
