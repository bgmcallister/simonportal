

# THE LEGACY OF BENNY BLUE: PART TWO (CHAPTERS 5 - 11)
# Written by Big G-Mack Engine (Gregory DeShawn McAllister)

### Chapter 5: Confessions and the Baby Blue Past

Walking back into that garage alone after dropping Bubba off at the bus station in San Bernardino felt like stepping into an empty vault. The desert silence has a way of amplified weight when your partner isn't around to chew the fat or argue over a socket size. I stood there in the quiet, looking down at my hands, still smelling the faint tang of gasoline and exhaust fumes. I reached into my pocket and pulled out that crumpled piece of paper that slick fella with the AMC Gremlin had handed me back in Indio. I stared at it for a minute, the paper feeling heavy between my fingers. The grief over losing pops was still a fresh wound, and now Bubba was heading off to some jungle across the world. It felt like the valley was shrinking around me. I walked over to the wall phone, rolled the long cord between my fingers, and dialed the number before my pride could talk me out of it. 

When Jerry answered, my voice sounded raspy, even to me. I told him I was ready to let Emerald Thunder go. The next morning, before the sun could even think about clearing the peaks, that battered blue-green Gremlin came rattling down my long, sandy driveway, kicking up a trail of white dust. Jerry stepped out, looking sharp as a tack despite his ride, that cartoonish red-haired pinup girl air freshener swaying lazily from his rearview mirror. 

He looked at me, gave a slow nod, and called me Greg. I told him most folks called me G-Mack, but he just smiled and stuck with Greg. Before we even talked cash, he looked toward the truck and asked about the boy who’d been with me the day before. When I told him Benny was on a bus to basic training, Jerry's smile faded into something deeper, something respectful. He told me he used to work down at the San Diego docks with Benny’s old man, Bernard. Hearing that name out here in the middle of the desert felt like a lightning strike. Jerry told me Bernard was one of the hardest, most honest men on the crew, a fella who could move twice as fast as guys half his age. 

But then the conversation took a turn that left a cold weight in my chest. I’d always thought Benny’s old man just packed up and moved away when we were kids. Jerry lowered his head, his voice dropping low into the desert quiet. He told me the system back then was a cruel animal, especially to a black man doing good work on those docks. Some boss pulled a frame job, accused Bernard of stealing things he never touched, and had the law take him away right in front of everyone. Bernard never got a chance to fight it. He was locked up, hidden away, and eventually died in a prison cell, forgotten by the world but not by the men who actually knew his heart. Knowing Benny grew up carrying the weight of an absent father without ever knowing the world had stolen him broke something inside me. 

Jerry handed me the cash—five hundred smackaroos, exactly what we’d agreed on. But before he climbed into the driver’s seat of Emerald Thunder, he asked me to hop in for one last spin. We rattled down the dirt path and hit the open highway, heading north about ten miles out into the wide open expanse of the Mojave. Jerry gunned the accelerator, the old K-10 howling against the wind, and he told me that old things don’t lie; they confess. He said every dent and scratch on a truck was a lesson someone had to pay for. 

He pulled up to an old dusty adobe-style house sitting completely solitary in the sand. Inside, it smelled of old coffee grounds and lemon polish, the walls lined with the ghosts of a different time. On the mantle sat a black-and-white photograph of a tall white man in overalls with his arm thrown around Bernard Blue. That tall man was Jerry’s father, Roy. Jerry reached into a drawer and unrolled a piece of wax paper, revealing a vintage photograph of my truck from a lifetime ago. Only she wasn’t green back then. She was a beautiful, gleaming shade of baby blue, fresh off the dealership lot, with Roy and Bernard wiping down the chrome. Jerry told me his father was the original owner, and that life has a strange way of coming full circle. I took the money, watched the green tailgate disappear down the highway, and felt the first chapter of my youth officially close.

### Chapter 6: The Language of Engines and Desert Therapy

Weeks started rolling by slow and heavy, the kind of days where the sun just bakes the earth and nothing seems to move. With Bubba down in Alabama for basic and Emerald Thunder gone, I had to rely on my old Chevy Biscayne to get around. It was a solid ride, slick lines and a low rumble, but it didn't carry the chaos or the brotherhood that the truck did. I’d sit on my porch in the evenings, watching the shadows stretch across the yard, wondering what kind of hell my best friend was learning to face across the ocean.
