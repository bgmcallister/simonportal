
THE LEGACY OF BENNY BLUE: IN THE LIFE AND TIMES OF BIG G-MACK MCALLISTER
Part One: Chapters 1 - 4 (The BGM Engine Rewrite)

Chapter 1: Desert Roots and the Deep Green Gift

Everybody knows "Bubba" by now. The shrimp-loving kid who stood side-by-side with Forrest Gump, matching him step for step until he fell on the muddy bank of some river out in Vietnam. But long before the uniform, long before his mind was completely wrapped around ocean tides, shrimp nets, and seafood recipes, and way before he ever met that skinny Gump kid at basic training... there was just Benny Blue. And there was the Coachella Valley. 

My name is Gregory DeShawn McAllister. Out here in the desert, nobody calls me that. They just know me as Big G-Mack. Born in 1950 right in the heart of Palm Springs, California, back when the valley floor felt like a brick oven and the streets constant smelled of melting asphalt and hungry ambition. My pops, Harold, was a working man. He laid brick all day long under that brutal sun, his hands getting rough and calloused like raw desert stone. My mama, Ernestine, she held our little house down like a queen guarding a throne, keeping everything tight and upright. 

Life back then wasn't what people think it was. When most folks hear "Palm Springs," their minds go straight to the high life. Turquoise swimming pools, VIP dining spots, expensive shopping down the strip, and maybe catching a glimpse of some Hollywood celebrity hiding behind dark sunglasses on vacation. But if you took a ride north of the main drag, past the neon signs, you ran right into the real machinery of the valley. A completely separate community built on the backs of working-class black and brown families trying to carve out a living. North Palm Springs and Desert Hot Springs. To me? That was home. 

And man, back then? Life felt golden. Hot summers that never seemed to break, riding Schwinn bicycles with chains that smelled heavily of grease and pure adventure, radios blasting the low-down harmonies of The Temptations, and a desert sky so wide and blue you could almost lose your whole self just staring up into it. Childhood was something beautiful, mostly because of my best friend, Benjamin. Benny was born in '49, just four months older than me, but he acted like my mentor and my big brother in every single way that mattered. His mama, Miss Loretta, ran a single-mom empire raising six kids by herself, but she always found a way to let me slide right under her wing too. 

Our family dinners were legendary. The smell of skillet-fried catfish would get so thick in the kitchen air it felt like it owned the place. Benny and I would spend our afternoons out by the old agricultural irrigation ponds and the banks of the Coachella Valley Canal, baking in the sun with our fishing poles until we pulled enough fish to bring home. The minute those catfish hit the hot lard and butter, making that heavy sizzle, the whole house would be licking their lips. There wasn't nothing on earth that beat that first bite when the cornmeal crust was still burning your tongue. 

That was where the dream started. Benny and I would sit out by those dirt banks, talking about the day we'd finally open up our own seafood joint. We'd fallen completely in love with the water after taking a couple of long ride-along trips out to the coast with Benny's uncle Felix on his commercial shrimping boat. The market prices on lobster and shrimp back then kept our pockets waiting, but man, we believed in it. Every massive moment in a man's life traces right back to those tiny little sparks, long before you have any clue how much weight the world is eventually going to ask you to carry.

I was fifteen years old when pops handed me the keys to Emerald Thunder. She was a deep green 1960 Chevy K-10 pickup truck. I'll never forget the first time I saw her gleaming through the afternoon desert haze. I turned around and gave pops the absolute biggest, tightest hug I had ever given anyone in my life. The man had worked brutal double night shifts for weeks on end just to save up every single penny to buy that steel. That truck—my truck—was finally ours. Pops didn't want me taking her out on the main roads until I officially had my license in my pocket, but Bubba and I were fifteen with massive dreams. Within two hours of sitting in that cab, we already had the next five years of her life completely mapped out. 

But fate has its own design, and it don't check with you first. That exact same night, pops went back into work for one last shift. He never made it back through the front door. A head-on car accident stole him away from us the exact same day he gave me that truck, leaving Emerald Thunder behind as a heavy vessel of unpayable love, lessons, and raw memory. 

Benny took the loss incredibly hard too. See, his own old man wasn't in the picture anymore, so Benny practically modeled his entire posture after my dad. We didn't talk about it out loud—that wasn't our way—but I knew it weighed heavy on his mind. Our dads had worked together down at the San Diego docks when we were just little toddlers, riding in the same car every single morning and laying brick together on the weekends. When pops eventually transitioned into laying brick full-time, he started hiring his own crew, while Benny's dad stayed back fixing up old cars on the weekends. I guess they just naturally drifted out of touch for a stretch of years, but our mamas kept the bridge alive, waitressing together at the local diner and forging a bond that echoed straight into our lives. From the exact moment pops passed, Emerald Thunder wasn't just my truck anymore. In my mind, and in everybody else's, she belonged to Benny just as much as she belonged to me.

Chapter 2: July Sparks and the Birth of a Moniker

When I look back now, I think about one specific afternoon when Benny and I were just two kids trying to figure out what respect, pride, and loyalty actually meant inside a garage that constantly hummed with the heat of old iron. It was the Fourth of July, 1966. The desert sun was blazing over the Coachella Valley like an open broiler, and the garage air was buzzing like it had its own heavy heartbeat. 

Benny and I had gotten into a real thick argument early that morning. It wasn't about engine parts this time; it was about him overstepping his bounds, acting like he was trying to be my dad. He kept breathing down my neck, telling me I was hanging around the wrong crowd down the block, warning me that I needed to watch my step because he wasn't about to sit back and watch me screw my life up. I shot right back at him, hot-headed, trying to make him see that I could handle my own business without a chaperone. Our voices kept climbing, hands waving over greasy car parts, until suddenly we just ran completely out of words. The suffocating heat of the day and the sheer weight of what we'd said just hung there between us like a physical wall. Benny leaned back against the wooden workbench, his arms crossed tight and his jaw locked hard. I leaned up against my old Chevy Biscayne, staring down at the chrome trim glinting in the sunlight, feeling the tension coil up in my chest. 

The silence stretched out forever, heavy and humming. Eventually, the heat started draining the anger out of me. I started talking a little softer, breaking down my side of things, trying to bridge the gap before pride could strangle the room. Benny didn't interrupt. He just stood there and listened, his hard edges softening up as I spoke. We didn't solve every single piece of it right then, but the silence was broken, and out here, that was always enough to get by.

We finally moved past the weight of it and decided to take the Biscayne out for a slow spin to catch whatever breeze the desert wanted to give us. Rolling down those cracked concrete streets, the black Chevy was gleaming under the sun, the chrome bumpers catching the light like little mirrors. 

“Man, look at that classic slick,” I murmured, nodding my head toward the smooth lines and the low, steady rumble of the motor. 

Benny just smiled, looking out the window. “Yeah... she’s definitely got one hell of a personality.” 

That was how we always talked about machinery back then. It wasn't just iron and stamped steel to us; everything had a personality. As we cruised down near the local park where a bunch of neighborhood kids were already setting off early fireworks, Benny completely froze up. His eyes locked onto a small group of teenagers hanging around the public water fountain. Right in the middle of them was Susie Anderson. She had her hair cut short, a beautiful, easy grin, and she was laughing up a storm at something her friend had just said. 

Now, Benny had been talking my ear off about Susie Anderson for at least a solid month, but the boy was absolutely terrified to actually walk up and ask her out. Seeing him caught flat-footed gave me an immediate idea. I was going to make damn sure Susie noticed him today. 

Before he could even figure out what I was doing, I pulled the Biscayne right up to the curb, threw her in park, and hopped clean out of the driver's seat. I walked around the hood, talking real loud so my voice would carry right over to the fountain, acting like the Chevy belonged completely to Benny. 

“Yeah man, she drives real nice," I hollered, giving him a heavy nudge as he stepped out of the passenger side. "You got yourself a beautiful machine here, brother.” 

Sure enough, Susie turned her head to look at the noise. She tilted her head, looked at the gleaming black car, and asked real clear, “Whose Chevy is that?” 

Benny was caught completely defenseless. His tongue tied up in knots, and before he could stumble over his own feet, I hollered right back to her, ”It’s my brother’s car!" 

Susie squinted through the sun glare, looking a little confused. The desert wind must have caught my voice, because she thought I said "Bubba's car." 

She called back, “Who’s Bubba!?” 

Benny, flustered as all hell with his face turning dark red, stammered out, “Oh... uh... that’s me.” 

I broke out laughing like absolute hell, and just like that, the concrete hardened on it. From that exact night forward, Benny wasn't just Benny anymore. He was Bubba. 

I handed him the keys, let him drive Susie all the way home in the Biscayne, and took the long walk back to my house on foot while the first evening fireworks started painting the sky in deep reds and greens. I watched those round taillights fade down Palm Canyon Drive, the red dots shrinking into the dark like fireflies slipping right through your fingers. The night air carried that thick desert perfume—a heavy mixture of spent gunpowder, cheap gasoline, and that dry heat that clings to your skin long after the sun goes down. I stuffed my hands deep into my pockets, letting the warmth of the evening settle over my shoulders. 

As I walked, I passed right by the newly built Tramway Gas Station, its sharp modernist roofline cutting into the night sky and its fluorescent bulbs buzzing faintly like they were still trying to wake up. Old man Reyes was inside, leaning lazy against the laminate counter, flipping through a newspaper and glancing up just long enough to give me a slow nod. The heavy smell of motor oil and fresh asphalt hung tight around the fuel pumps, and I remember thinking how strange it was that something so brand new could instantly feel like it had been sitting in the neighborhood forever. 

North of the station, the open pass stretched out wide and empty, completely silent. I looked up toward the jagged silhouette of Mount San Jacinto, watching the tiny, blinking lights of the modern Aerial Tramway slowly gliding up the sheer rock cliffs like a distant star suspended on an invisible string, throwing off quiet sparks of silver against the dark mountain face. 

I turned east onto the dirt road that led straight toward my block, my boots kicking up little clouds of fine sand that caught the pale moonlight. Off to the side, a massive, jagged scar of turned earth marked exactly where the state crews were carving out the new interstate highway—the one folks around town were whispering about, calling it the "future road" that would eventually become the I-10. It didn't look like the streets we grew up on; there weren't any curbs or familiar landmarks yet, just the raw promise of a path that hadn't fully arrived. By the time I reached the edge of my front yard, the last echoes of the fireworks had completely died out, replaced by the deep hum of evening cicadas and the occasional distant rumble of an engine echoing out of the canyon. That night carried a strange kind of weight, a feeling that the ground beneath our feet was shifting into something else, even if I couldn't put a proper name to it yet. Somewhere between the blinking lights on the mountain peaks and the dark expanse of the canyon, I felt the steady pull of home, the familiar hum of the yard, and the phantom memory of pops hollering from the porch telling me to get inside before the dark caught me. 

The "future road" eventually arrived, and with it, came the spring of 1967.

Chapter 3: The Future Road and the Marking of Steel

The morning hung soft and pale over the Coachella Valley, a low haze stretching between the mountains as if the desert itself was dragging its feet to wake up. Emerald Thunder was sitting clean inside my garage, her green paint cool to the touch. Bubba and I had just gotten back from catching an early breakfast at the diner where our mamas used to pull double shifts together. The sun had barely broken over the horizon, spilling a thick, honeyed glow across the dirt streets, when we decided to crack open the toolbox and do a little work on the truck's exhaust before Bubba had to leave town. 

Bubba had a white paper ticket folded tight inside his shirt pocket—a one-way bus ride leaving out of San Bernardino, carrying him straight toward a green uniform. The war overseas had finally come knocking right on his front door, and even though he'd taken the draft notice with that same calm, slow grin of his, I could feel the massive weight sitting right between us in the quiet space of the garage.

We were elbow-deep in the Chevy's engine bay, tinkering with the manifolds and laughing about old times, when Bubba suddenly wandered away toward the passenger side of the cab. 

“Ay, Benny, you see where that hose clamp went?” I called out, leaning over the fender. 

I didn't get an answer, just a faint, sharp scraping noise against the steel. I walked around the front bumper to see what he was up to, and my chest immediately went tight. Bubba was kneeling down by the open passenger door, digging a pocket knife right into the green paint of the interior door panel. 

“WHAT?... WHAT IN THE HELL ARE YOU DOIN', YOU IDIOT?!” I shouted, half in raw anger and half in absolute disbelief. 

Bubba jumped a bit, startled, holding his hands up quick with the blade still out, trying to find his words. “G-Mack, hold up, brother, hold up! I just... I just wanted to leave my mark on Thunder. You know, G... just... in case.” 

I shook my head, my jaw tight. “Aw, damn it, Bubba... Aw, man... you know exactly how much this truck means to me. Pops gave me this steel. What the hell are you thinking, man?” 

I stepped closer and looked down at the carved-up panel. I ran my thumb down the sharp, rough edges of the scarred steel, feeling the deep groove he'd cut into the green paint. Then, I did something I rarely ever did back in those hot-headed days. I just stopped. I paused, and I let my mind sit still for a single second. And out here, sometimes a second is all it takes for the world to flip right side up. 

I took my hand off the scratch, a slow grin breaking through my face, and I looked right at him. “Hey, you know what, Bubba?” 

He looked up at me, real uneasy, shifting his weight. “Yeah, G-Mack?” 

I pointed right back down at the scarred door panel. “Go ahead and finish it, buddy.” 

Bubba’s slow grin widened out until it took over his whole face. He leaned back in and traced the last sharp line of his name right into the green paint of Emerald Thunder. The raspy song of that steel blade against the metal echoed through the quiet garage like a holy hymn. I ran a heavy hand down my face, shaking my head at my own sudden change of heart, but we were both smiling like a couple of damn fools. We just sat there on the concrete, looking right at each other, taking the whole moment in. We didn't say a word, but we said more in those smiles than most men say in an entire lifetime. Then we both reached out and hugged each other with the kind of bone-crushing strength that mirrored the old bear hugs pops used to give us when we were just boys. 

I broke it up first, clearing my throat. “Alright, ya big lug, that’s enough of that,” I muttered, quietly sniffling back a stray tear before he could see it. 

The garage went quiet for a minute, the smell of penetrating oil and old exhaust hanging thick between us. “Man... Benny... You are lucky as all hell my pops ain't standing here to see you scratching up a clean Chevy door like that.” 

“Shoot, G... Pops gave it to you, right? That makes it yours. And you? You're my brother. I just wanted to leave a real piece of me right here before I head out across the water. You think I’m gonna travel halfway around the world and let Emerald Thunder forget about me?” He paused, looking back down at the silver scratches. “Hey, you know, Big G... come to think of it? For all those reasons, I think this here etching would be... 'pops-approved'! Ha!” 

For a heartbeat, the warm desert air wrapped tight around the garage, filling the room with a deep, settled quiet. I couldn't help but smile. That boy always had a way of turning the heaviest situations into something pure and positive. I didn't think much of it back then, but looking back now? He was exactly right. Pops was a strict man, lived his whole life by a rigid set of rules, but he prioritized relationships over everything else. He wore his heart right on his sleeve and always preached the gospel of brotherhood and compassion. Being the only real, stand-up black man in Bubba's life after his own dad disappeared, Bubba had clung to my daddy's words like they were written in stone. Pops lived the way a man should live. Didn't matter the circumstances, didn't matter what shade of skin you carried or what kind of beef you thought you had with him, he treated every single neighbor with the exact same quiet respect. In a stereotyping society that constant tried to put men like us in a box, pops didn't give a damn about any of it. When he went, Bubba took it just as hard as I did. Every once in a while, even now, I'll catch myself looking at a certain angle—maybe watching someone tie a knot in a fishing line or cracking a stubborn bolt loose—and I'll see the phantom shadow of pops living right inside Bubba's movements. 

“Hey, yo, G-Mack!” Bubba hollered from across the floor, breaking my daydream. “Man, look at this beautiful truck.” 

I let out a slow breath, walking over and giving him a light shove away from the fender. “Yeah, man... for a shit-box Chevy, she’s looking pretty slick.” 

We laughed low and easy together, staring down at those silver letters etched permanently into Emerald Thunder—a memory fixed in solid steel.

Chapter 4: One Last Cruise and the Bus to Basic

Our pal Bugsy called the shop later that afternoon, telling us that Thunder's new dog-dish rims had finally come in on the freight delivery, so we washed the grease off our hands and made the long trip down the highway into Indio. Now let me tell you something, young blood—if you ever want some bonafide, grade-A mechanical work done for a fair price out here, Bugsy’s Speed and Lube is still up and running at that exact same spot on Van Buren Street. 

Anyway, we pulled up to Bugsy's garage just as the sun was starting to duck behind the jagged mountain peaks, painting the whole town of Indio in a deep, warm golden haze. After a fresh rim swap, Emerald Thunder was looking better than she ever had against that mountain backdrop. Under the fading glow of the setting sun, those new dog-dish bullet-hole rims were catching the light, fresh and shining. Bubba was bouncing on his heels, his eyes darting between me and the chrome. 

Right then, out of the corner of my eye, I saw a beat-up, sputtering AMC Gremlin rattle its way down the street and pull right up to the curb. The driver climbed out dressed sharper than his ride, a cartoonish air freshener of a red-haired pinup girl swaying wildly from his rearview mirror. 

He walked up to us with his arms crossed, a smug, streetwise grin pasted on his face. “Listen up, boy. I got five hundred smackaroos right here in my pocket, right now. You ain't gonna get another offer like that for an old truck like this,” he said, talking big like a cat who just caught a mouse. 

I took immediate offense. I mean, who in the hell did this Billy Johnson-looking guy think he was stepping onto our block? 

I stood my ground, my own streetwise mask locked tight. “You can save your breath, man. Ain't no way under God's sun I’m selling this truck.” 

He kept pushing, walking closer with his hand extended. “Come on, kid! Just take the number. Think about it.” 

I shook my head, letting out a soft, dry laugh. “Listen to me, pal—I’ve got a mountain of phone numbers back at the house from guys offering me all sorts of wild prices. Four hundred, eight hundred, a dollar fifty. If you knew the real story I have with this girl right here, you’d save your ink. She is NOT. FOR. SALE.” 

The man threw his hands up in the air, backing down. “Whatever, fine, have it your way, kid. Just keep the paper. Who knows, you might wake up tomorrow and change your mind.” 

I took the scrap of paper out of his hand, crumpled it into a tight ball, and stuffed it straight into my pocket. Bubba nudged my elbow, shaking his head with a slow chuckle. “Man, you always keeping your pride locked down, huh? Ain't nobody ever gonna buy her from you.” 

“Damn right, buster brown—ain't nobody ever touching this steel. She’s bonafide family.” 

Bubba looked back at the green paint, his voice dropping low. “Man... if your old man could see what you done with this girl, he’d be so damn proud of you, G.” 

I nodded, the streetwise mask slipping off for a second to show a soft, genuine smile. “Bubba...” 

“Wassup, G?” he asked, looking at me like he didn't see the weight of what he'd just said. 

“Brother... you know damn well you’ve put more actual love and hard work into this old girl than I ever have,” I told him straight. 

He chuckled, looking out at the mountains. “Ha! Okay, G-Mack, how about we just say we both put the exact same amount of LOVE into her? See, then I ain't lying.” 

And the beautiful thing was, he wasn't lying either. I knew with my entire being that boy loved Emerald Thunder just as much as I did, and anybody who knew our history would tell you the exact same thing—it was Bubba's truck, just as much as it was mine. 

“Hey, Bubba.” 

“Yeah, G?” 

“You ready for one last cruise, buddy?” 

He looked at me, that big, signature grin breaking across his face. “You know it, Big G-Mack. Let’s do this.” 

The streetlights of Indio started flickering on one by one as Bubba climbed into the passenger side, laughing softly to himself and shaking his head. I slapped the steel roof of the cab, feeling the quiet weight of pride and protection settle over us. The sun continued its long descent behind the peaks, casting stretched shadows across the cracked asphalt as Emerald Thunder rumbled through the desert streets worn smooth by time. The loud laughter eventually ebbed away, leaving the deep warmth of shared memories hanging in the cab like an invisible blanket. 

Bubba leaned his head back against the seat, smirking, but there was a quiet, heavy darkness sitting behind his eyes. “Man... you ever think about all the crazy stuff we been through? All the dumb choices, all the times we almost got ourselves thrown in the back of a squad car?” 

“Damn it, Bubba... I been thinking about it all day," I said, keeping my eyes fixed on the white lines of the highway. "Ain't nobody else on this earth I’d rather have done all that mischief with. You're my brother, man. Blood or no blood.” 

“Yeah, man... but I’ve thought about that before," he murmured, looking out at the dark desert brush. "Ain't we shared enough actual blood between us by now to be considered real blood brothers?” 

I chuckled, my eyes widening a bit as I looked over at him. “Hey, buddy, you got a damn swell point there!” 

“And hey, G—you remember how we used to sneak out to those old irrigation ponds for fishing? That one time... oh man... that one specific time we caught that monster catfish and brought it back to your kitchen?” 

“Ha!” I hollered, the details hitting my mind like a flash. “Yeah, man, holy shit! I haven't thought about that in years! My lord... we didn't even realize what time it was. My pops walked right through the front door from a long shift just as the lard finished heating up... and YOU, Mister Smooth Move Benny, slipped right on the spilled cooking oil, launching that entire, giant, undercooked fish directly into my pops' face like a circus clown with a whipped pie!” 

I shook my head, laughing so hard my ribs ached. “I'll tell you what, Bubba, I have never in my entire life seen such a confused, shocked, purely pissed-off look on a man's face as I did that day!” 

Bubba buried his face in his rough hands, laughing up a storm but still carrying that tiny bit of old regret for what my dad must have thought of him in that moment. The heavy laughter rolled around the cab until it slowly faded back into a quiet, reflective silence. 

“Hey, G-Mack... you know what would really be something?” Bubba said, breaking the quiet. 

“Yeah? What’s that?” I asked. “You gonna finish the thought or what?” 

“Well, look here, I been thinking, G... wouldn't it be something if I went all the way over to Vietnam... and I fought in the jungle... and I came back home without a single split hair on my head...” 

“Yeah? And? Will you just spit it out, Benny?” 

“...I came home completely healthy and uninjured... just to be killed right in my sleep by the ghost of your dead daddy coming back to serve me his final revenge for that catfish?” 

I broke out laughing so hard I had to grip the steering wheel with both hands just to keep Emerald Thunder from veering clean off the dark road. 

Before long, the open desert receded, and the streetlights of San Bernardino swallowed us up. The big Greyhound bus was just pulling into the concrete lot when we finally rolled to a stop. Bubba’s canvas duffel bag hung heavy over his shoulder, his calm, signature grin trying its absolute best to mask the storm of fear I could feel raging right behind his eyes. 

“You got this, man,” I said, stepping up and slapping him hard on the shoulder. “One last ride, buddy. Don't you dare make me cry out here, alright?” 

“Ha! G-Mack... always trying to act tough,” he murmured, ducking quick under the hand I gave him. “I’ll be back, don't you worry. You’ll see.” 

Bubba looked right at me. He smiled big, but for the first time in his life, his eyes looked completely terrified. “Hey, buddy... You’re good.” 

Before I could even get the last word out of my mouth, Bubba threw his huge arms around me, locking me into one of those legendary, bone-crushing hugs. We didn't need to say another word. He turned around, walked up the rubber steps, and disappeared straight into the bus. 

I stood there in the concrete lot for a long time, Emerald Thunder parked right beside me like an old soldier resting after a long battle. Inside the bus, the military atmosphere hit him immediate. I could hear the bus driver barking instructions down the aisle, “GET IN YOUR SEAT!” 

Bubba muttered something under his breath, ducking low into a vacant seat near the very back. I couldn't see his face clearly through the tinted glass, but I could sense the quiet, steady way he was sizing up the other passengers around him. He sat by himself, keeping his hands busy getting his duffel bag into order. 

After a couple of minutes, a tall, skinny white boy climbed up the steps and started shuffling down the aisle. 

Bubba looked up and said to him, “You can sit down if you want to.” 

They talked back and forth for a brief minute about where they were headed. Then Bubba leaned in and asked, “You ever been on a real shrimp boat?” 

The skinny kid blinked, responding real slow, “No, but I been on a real big boat.” 

Bubba just looked at him for a solid second, reading him. “I’m talkin’ about a shrimp catchin’ boat. I’ve been fishin' and messin' around on shrimp boats all my life. I started out on my uncle’s boat—that’s my mother’s brother—when I was about maybe nine.” 

Watching from the asphalt, I knew Bubba was just happy to have a soul to talk to, to keep the dark thoughts at bay. 

“I was just lookin’ into buyin’ a boat of my own and got drafted," Bubba kept right on talking, true to his nature. "My given name is Benjamin Buford Blue. People call me Bubba... Just like one of them redneck boys. Can you believe that?” 

The white kid responded in that steady, unforgettable cadence, “My name’s Forrest Gump. ...People call me... um... Forrest Gump.” 

Bubba didn't miss a beat. “Yeah, I know everything there is to know about the seafood business. Matter of fact, I’m goin’ into the business myself after I get out the Army... With my best buddy, Greg.” 

The Gump kid, awkward as all hell, just nodded. “Okay.” 

I stood there shaking my head as the bus finally ground its gears and started pulling out of the station. I knew right then, with everything inside me, that this strange kid from Alabama was about to have a friend for life.
